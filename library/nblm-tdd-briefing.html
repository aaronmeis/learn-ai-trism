Briefing Document: Artificial Intelligence Trust, Risk, and Security Management (AI-TRISM) Architecture, Standards Alignment, and Operational Governance

1. Executive Summary

Enterprise adoption of Artificial Intelligence (AI)—spanning Large Language Models (LLMs), Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), autonomous software agents, and classical Machine Learning (ML) models—presents operational, safety, and security vulnerabilities that conventional Information Technology (IT) controls cannot mitigate. AI systems exhibit non-deterministic behavior, rely on dynamic datasets subject to drift, accept unvalidated natural-language inputs capable of executing attacks, and operate with varying degrees of autonomy.

AI Trust, Risk, and Security Management (AI-TRISM) provides a unified architectural reference model and operational governance framework designed to establish trust, manage socio-technical risks, and enforce security controls across the complete AI lifecycle.

This document synthesizes core architectural blueprints, primary governance standards (NIST AI RMF 1.0 / NIST AI 100-1, NIST AI 600-1, ISO/IEC 42001:2023), statutory mandates (EU AI Act Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), and practical threat models (OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025).

1. NIST AI RMF 1.0 (NIST AI 100-1)

Published by NIST in January 2023 (authored by Elham Tabassi under the National Artificial Intelligence Initiative Act of 2020, P.L. 116-283), the AI Risk Management Framework provides a voluntary, rights-preserving, non-sector-specific, and use-case agnostic standard to address AI-specific risks.

Core Functions
  • GOVERN: Cross-cutting function infused throughout all AI risk management processes. Cultivates risk management culture, defines organizational risk tolerance, establishes accountability structures, manages third-party supply chain risks, and structures transparent decision-making.
  • MAP: Establishes context, categorizes system tasks, identifies intended benefits and potential non-monetary costs, maps socio-technical impacts, and evaluates initial go/no-go commissioning decisions.
  • MEASURE: Employs quantitative, qualitative, and empirical methodologies to test, evaluate, verify, and validate (TEVV) AI system trustworthiness, safety, security, fairness, and scientific validity prior to deployment and during production monitoring.
  • MANAGE: Allocates risk management resources to prioritized risks identified during MAP and MEASURE, implementing risk response plans (mitigate, transfer, avoid, accept), disengagement protocols, and post-deployment monitoring.
The Seven Trustworthiness Characteristics

+---------------+ +---------------+ +---------------+ +---------------+ +---------------+
| SAFE | | SECURE AND | | EXPLAINABLE & | | PRIVACY- | | FAIR - HARMFUL|
| (Protection | | RESILIENT | | INTERPRETABLE | | ENHANCED | | BIAS MANAGED |

+---------------+ +---------------+ +---------------+ +---------------+ +---------------+ | | | | | +------------------+------------------+------------------+------------------+ | v +-----------------------------------+ | VALID AND RELIABLE | | (Foundational Condition) | +-----------------------------------+
  1. Valid and Reliable: Foundational condition of trustworthiness. Validation confirms fulfillment of intended requirements; reliability ensures consistent accuracy and robustness under expected conditions throughout system lifetime.
  2. Safe: Systems must not, under defined conditions, cause states where human life, health, property, or the environment are endangered.
  3. Secure and Resilient: Systems must withstand unexpected adverse events, maintain confidentiality, integrity, and availability, resist adversarial machine learning attacks (e.g., evasion, model extraction, data poisoning), and degrade gracefully.
  4. Accountable and Transparent: Accountable practices depend on meaningful transparency regarding data provenance, model architecture, intended use, and operational decisions, allowing actionable redress for negative outputs.
  5. Explainable and Interpretable: Explainability addresses how a model operates technically; interpretability addresses why an output occurred within a specific functional context.
  6. Privacy-Enhanced: Enforces privacy norms (anonymity, confidentiality, agency) using Privacy-Enhancing Technologies (PETs), de-identification, and data minimization.
  7. Fair — with Harmful Bias Managed: Addresses systemic bias (societal/organizational norms), computational/statistical bias (data sampling errors), and human-cognitive bias (perception/oversight errors) to advance equity.

3. EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689)

Adopted June 13, 2024 (and amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 on July 8, 2026), the EU AI Act establishes a legally binding harmonized legal framework across the European Union:

  • Statutory Purpose (Article 1): Promotes human-centric, trustworthy AI while ensuring high-level protection of health, safety, fundamental rights, democracy, the rule of law, and environmental protection.
  • Scope & Extra-Territorial Reach (Article 2): Applies to providers placing AI systems or general-purpose AI models on the EU market (regardless of whether established in the EU or a third country), deployers located within the EU, and third-country providers/deployers whose AI output is used in the EU.
  • Statutory Exclusions (Article 2): Explicitly excludes AI systems developed or used exclusively for military, defence, or national security purposes; scientific research and development; non-commercial testing/research prior to market placement; open-source licensed AI systems (unless classified as high-risk or prohibited under Article 5/50); and purely personal, non-professional activity by natural persons.
  • Definition of AI System (Article 3(1)): A machine-based system designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from input how to generate outputs (predictions, content, recommendations, decisions) influencing physical or virtual environments.

3. Important Quotes with Context

The following quotes represent primary authority across governance standards, regulatory legal texts, and operational threat frameworks.

1. On the Definition and Autonomy of AI Systems

"The AI RMF refers to an AI system as an engineered or machine-based system that can, for a given set of objectives, generate outputs such as predictions, recommendations, or decisions influencing real or virtual environments. AI systems are designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy..."

— NIST AI RMF 1.0 (NIST AI 100-1), Executive Summary, Page 1

  • Context and Significance: This quote establishes the foundational technical definition adopted by NIST (derived from OECD and ISO/IEC 22989:2023). It emphasizes that autonomy and environmental influence are core characteristics of AI systems, establishing why deterministic software testing methodologies are insufficient for managing AI risks.

2. On How AI Risks Differ from Traditional Software Risks

"While there are myriad standards and best practices to help organizations mitigate the risks of traditional software or information-based systems, the risks posed by AI systems are in many ways unique... AI systems, for example, may be trained on data that can change over time, sometimes significantly and unexpectedly, affecting system functionality and trustworthiness in ways that are hard to understand. AI systems and the contexts in which they are deployed are frequently complex, making it difficult to detect and respond to failures when they occur. AI systems are inherently socio-technical in nature..."

— NIST AI RMF 1.0 (NIST AI 100-1), Executive Summary, Page 1

  • Context and Significance: Explains the foundational rationale for creating an AI-specific management framework. It highlights that data drift, operational inscrutability, socio-technical interactions, and system complexity create emergent failure modes that conventional IT security frameworks fail to address.

3. On Navigating Tradeoffs Between Trustworthiness Characteristics

"Addressing AI trustworthiness characteristics individually will not ensure AI system trustworthiness; tradeoffs are usually involved, rarely do all characteristics apply in every setting, and some will be more or less important in any given situation... For example, in certain scenarios tradeoffs may emerge between optimizing for interpretability and achieving privacy. In other cases, organizations might face a tradeoff between predictive accuracy and interpretability."

— NIST AI RMF 1.0 (NIST AI 100-1), Section 3, Page 12

  • Context and Significance: Highlights that AI risk management is an exercise in optimizing competing values rather than satisfying absolute technical checklists. Practitioners must document contextual trade-offs—such as sacrificing raw accuracy for explainability, or balancing privacy-enhancing techniques against model precision.

4. On Statutory Purpose under the European AI Regulation

"The purpose of this Regulation is to improve the functioning of the internal market and promote the uptake of human-centric and trustworthy artificial intelligence (AI), while ensuring a high level of protection of health, safety, fundamental rights enshrined in the Charter, including democracy, the rule of law and environmental protection, against the harmful effects of AI systems in the Union and supporting innovation."

— EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 1(1)

  • Context and Significance: Defines the statutory legal mandate governing AI deployment in the European Economic Area. It establishes that legal compliance requires balancing commercial innovation against enforceable protection of fundamental rights, safety, and democracy.

5. On the Harmonized Statutory Definition of an AI System

"'AI system' means a machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments;"

— EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 3(1)

  • Context and Significance: The legally binding definition establishing jurisdiction under Union law. It explicitly incorporates "adaptiveness after deployment" and "inference capability" as core attributes distinguishing regulated AI systems from traditional rule-based algorithms.

6. On Limitations of Legacy Security Frameworks Against Machine Learning Vulnerabilities

"At the same time, guidance available before publication of this AI RMF does not comprehensively address many AI system risks. For example, existing frameworks and guidance are unable to: adequately manage the problem of harmful bias in AI systems; confront the challenging risks related to generative AI; comprehensively address security concerns related to evasion, model extraction, membership inference, availability, or other machine learning attacks; and account for the complex attack surface of AI systems..."

— NIST AI RMF 1.0 (NIST AI 100-1), Appendix B, Page 39

  • Context and Significance: Explicitly details the security gaps present in legacy cybersecurity frameworks (e.g., standard NIST CSF or ISO 27001). It serves as standard justification for deploying dedicated AI-TRISM control capabilities—such as AI firewalls, model red-teaming, and agent identity brokers.

4. Actionable Insights and Decision Frameworks

1. Decision Rules for Architecture Realization

  • Rule 1 (Policy Separation): When designing runtime policy enforcement, choose an out-of-model Policy Engine ($C4$) because AI models are non-deterministic and cannot be trusted to reliably enforce safety policies upon themselves.
  • Rule 2 (Agent Identity Isolation): When deploying autonomous AI agents, choose dedicated workload identities with short-lived, scoped tokens ($C4$) because granting agents shared or persistent service-account credentials introduces excessive agency risk (OWASP LLM06).
  • Rule 3 (Retrieval Data Isolation): When connecting vector stores to RAG applications, choose inline input inspection at the gateway ($CP4$) because untrusted external documents introduce indirect prompt injection vulnerabilities (OWASP LLM01).
  • Rule 4 (Deployment Sequence): When rolling out AI-TRISM capabilities, choose to stand up the Asset Catalog ($C1$) and Evidence Store ($C8$) prior to enforcing runtime gateway blocks because enforcement policies cannot be accurately scoped without inventory context and audit baseline capture.

Milestone 1: Inventory and Governance Baseline (Days 1–30)

  • Action 1.1: Instantiate the central AI Asset Registry ($C1$) and enforce mandatory registration hooks in CI/CD pipelines for all enterprise models, LLM applications, and autonomous agents.
  • Action 1.2: Formalize executive governance by appointing an AI Risk Owner and defining organizational risk tolerance thresholds across operational business units (NIST GOVERN 1.3).
  • Action 1.3: Adopt the NIST AI RMF 1.0 core functions (GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE) and crosswalk existing control objectives against ISO/IEC 42001:2023 management system clauses.

Milestone 2: Runtime Visibility and Data Lineage (Days 31–60)

  • Action 2.1: Deploy the Runtime Gateway ($C4$) in monitor-only mode across all LLM egress boundaries to inspect prompts, completions, and tool executions without impacting production traffic.
  • Action 2.2: Establish the cryptographically append-only Evidence Store ($C8$) to capture interaction records, policy decisions, and model approvals (ISO 42001 Control B.6.2.8).
  • Action 2.3: Execute data classification scans across training sets, vector stores, and retrieval databases to map lineage and enforce privacy handling rules ($C2, C7$).

Milestone 3: Active Enforcement and Automated TEVV (Days 61–90)

  • Action 3.1: Integrate the Evaluation and Red-Team Harness ($C3$) into build pipelines to gate deployments on automated safety, bias, and robustness benchmark passes.
  • Action 3.2: Deploy the Agent Identity and Access Broker ($C4$) to enforce least-privilege workload isolation, short-lived tokens, and human approval gates for agent tool executions (OWASP LLM06).
  • Action 3.3: Switch the Runtime Gateway to active enforce mode, blocking prompt injections (LLM01), sensitive info disclosures (LLM02), and unbounded API consumption (LLM10).
