Comprehensive Study Guide: AI Trust, Risk, and Security Management (AI-TRISM) & AI Governance Frameworks

1. Core Concepts & Analytical Frameworks

1.1 AI-TRISM Pedagogy and Programmatic Framing

AI Trust, Risk, and Security Management (AI-TRISM) is a governance, architectural, and operational paradigm designed to ensure AI system reliability, trustworthiness, security, privacy, and regulatory compliance.

Pedagogy and Learning Structure

As outlined in the One-Day Mastery curriculum slugged ai-trism, working fluency in AI-TRISM can be acquired through a timed 350-minute, seven-block pedagogical framework:

Block Order Block Name Duration Primary Objective & Exit Criteria
Block 1 Core Concepts 60 min Lock vocabulary and baseline definitions across primary standards.
Block 2 Mental Models 45 min Chunk the domain by contrasting market categories, voluntary frameworks, management standards, and binding law.
Block 3 Active Practice 90 min Perform deliberate desk exercises mapping controls to risk scenarios (followed by a 15–20 min break).
Block 4 Teach It 30 min Deliver a 5-minute teach-back explaining the architecture without notes.
Block 5 Edge Cases 45 min Analyze failure modes, privilege escalations, and guardrail bypasses (followed by a 10 min break).

Block 7 Application 60 min Design and defend an enterprise AI-TRISM reference architecture in a high-stakes scenario.

Architectural Scope and Boundaries

The operational boundaries of AI-TRISM mandate the design and defense of a multi-layered reference architecture. This scope explicitly includes: * Explainability and Monitoring: Real-time operational tracking, behavioral logging, and model output interpretability. * ModelOps and Lifecycle Management: End-to-end governance of data, models, and application code from design through decommissioning. * AI Application Security: Guardrailing, privilege control, identity management for AI agents, and defense against machine learning attacks (e.g., OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025). * Privacy and Compliance: Data minimization, privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs), statutory alignment, and fundamental rights impact assessments.

1.2 NIST AI Risk Management Framework (NIST AI 100-1 / AI RMF 1.0)

Published in January 2023 by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (authored by Elham Tabassi) pursuant to the National Artificial Intelligence Initiative Act of 2020 (P.L. 116-283), the NIST AI RMF 1.0 provides a voluntary, non-sector-specific, rights-preserving, and use-case agnostic framework to manage AI risks and cultivate trustworthy AI systems.

Definition of AI System and Socio-Technical Risk

  • AI System: A machine-based system that can, for a given set of objectives, generate outputs such as predictions, recommendations, or decisions influencing real or virtual environments, operating with varying levels of autonomy and potential post-deployment adaptiveness (adapted from OECD 2019 and ISO/IEC 22989:2022).
  • Risk: The composite measure of an event's probability of occurring and the magnitude or degree of its consequences (ISO 31000:2018 / OMB Circular A-130:2016). AI risks emerge from complex socio-technical interactions between technical code/models, organizational norms, user behaviors, and societal context.
  • Residual Risk: The unmitigated risk remaining after risk treatment (ISO GUIDE 73).

The Four Core Functions of NIST AI RMF

The Core of the NIST AI RMF is organized into four continuous, iterative functions:

 ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ GOVERN │ │ (Cross-cutting culture & structure) │ └────────────────────┬────────────────────┘ │ ┌─────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────┐ ▼ ▼ ▼ ┌─────────────┐ ┌─────────────┐ ┌─────────────┐ │ MAP │ ────────────> │ MEASURE │ ────────────> │ MANAGE │ │ (Context) │ │ (TEVV) │ │(Treatment) │ └─────────────┘ └─────────────┘ └─────────────┘
  1. GOVERN: A cross-cutting function infused throughout all AI risk management activities. It establishes organizational culture, executive accountability, risk tolerance thresholds, workforce diversity (DEIA), inventory mechanisms (GOVERN 1.6), safe decommissioning procedures (GOVERN 1.7), and third-party/supply-chain risk policies (GOVERN 6).
  2. MAP: Establishes context, categorizes the system, defines intended purposes, assesses socio-technical impacts, and maps business/legal risks across internal and third-party components. Outcomes from MAP inform the initial go/no-go commissioning decision and serve as the foundation for MEASURE and MANAGE.
  3. MEASURE: Employs quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-method tools for Test, Evaluation, Verification, and Validation (TEVV). It assesses performance, tracks trustworthiness metrics, monitors production behavior, and evaluates safety, security, privacy, and bias prior to deployment and continuously during operation.
  4. MANAGE: Allocates risk management resources to mapped and measured risks. It prioritizes risk treatments (mitigating, transferring, avoiding, or accepting), addresses residual risks, establishes emergency deactivation/disengagement mechanisms (MANAGE 2.4), monitors pre-trained models and third-party components, and maintains incident response and post-deployment monitoring plans.

The Seven Trustworthiness Characteristics

NIST AI RMF articulates seven interconnected characteristics of trustworthy AI systems. Valid & Reliable forms the foundational baseline, while Accountable & Transparent cross-cuts all characteristics:

┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ ACCOUNTABLE & TRANSPARENT │
├───────────────┬──────────────┬──────────────────┬──────────────────────┤
│ SAFE │ SECURE & │ EXPLAINABLE & │ PRIVACY-ENHANCED │
│ │ RESILIENT │ INTERPRETABLE │ │
├───────────────┴──────────────┴──────────────────┴──────────────────────┤
│ FAIR — WITH HARMFUL BIAS MANAGED │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ VALID & RELIABLE │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  1. Valid and Reliable: Validation confirms that intended requirements are fulfilled (ISO 9000:2015). Reliability ensures error-free execution under specified conditions over time (ISO/IEC TS 5723:2022). Incorporates accuracy (closeness to true values) and robustness (ability to maintain performance under unexpected circumstances).
  2. Safe: Systems must not, under defined conditions, endanger human life, health, property, or the environment (ISO/IEC TS 5723:2022). Requires real-time monitoring, fail-safe mechanisms, and human intervention capabilities.
  3. Secure and Resilient: Security maintains confidentiality, integrity, and availability against intentional attacks (e.g., data poisoning, evasion, model extraction, membership inference). Resilience is the ability to withstand unexpected adverse events or environmental changes and degrade gracefully.
  4. Accountable and Transparent: Transparency provides appropriate information to actors regarding system operation and decisions. Accountability relies on transparency and clear organizational governance to allow for meaningful redress and decision attribution.
  5. Explainable and Interpretable: Explainability represents the underlying operational mechanisms of the system ("how" a decision was reached). Interpretability communicates the meaning of outputs within the user's operational context ("why" a decision matters).
  6. Privacy-Enhanced: Safeguards human autonomy, identity, and dignity using privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs), data minimization, de-identification, and aggregation.
  7. Fair — with Harmful Bias Managed: Promotes equity and equality while mitigating harmful bias. Addresses three major categories of bias: * Systemic Bias: Ingrained in historical datasets, societal structures, and organizational practices. * Computational & Statistical Bias: Derived from unrepresentative sampling or algorithmic errors. * Human-Cognitive Bias: Errors in how humans perceive, design, or interpret AI inputs and outputs.

Socio-Technical AI Lifecycle Dimensions and Actors

NIST adapts the OECD lifecycle model into five socio-technical dimensions: * Application Context: Defining objectives and context of use. * Data and Input: Collection, cleaning, and metadata profiling. * AI Model: Model selection, building, training, tuning, and internal testing. * Task and Output: Integration into operational workflows and end-user interactions. * People & Planet: Central dimension representing human rights, societal well-being, and environmental sustainability.

Key AI actors are separated across these dimensions. As a structural best practice (Figure 3), actors building and using models must be separated from independent actors performing TEVV functions.

AI System Risks vs. Traditional Software Risks (Appendix B)

AI systems introduce novel risk profiles not fully addressed by traditional software governance frameworks: * Data Reliance & Context Detachment: AI systems depend heavily on massive data volumes where ground truth may be non-existent or stale relative to deployment settings. * Emergent Properties & Scale: Billions or trillions of decision points introduce inscrutability, making failure modes hard to predict or reproduce. * Drift Dynamics: Data drift, model drift, and concept drift require continuous post-deployment recalibration and maintenance triggers. * Complex Attack Surfaces: Vulnerability to specialized ML attacks (evasion, data poisoning, membership inference, model exfiltration). * Pre-trained Model Dependencies: Transfer learning and off-label foundation model usage create downstream statistical uncertainties and inherited bias. * Environmental Impact: Massive computational infrastructure demands create significant carbon and energy footprints.

1.4 Legal Framework: The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 as amended by 2026/1744)

Enacted on June 13, 2024 (published July 12, 2024; amended July 8, 2026 by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744), the EU AI Act establishes harmonized, binding statutory rules across the European Union.

Subject Matter and Scope (Articles 1 & 2)

  • Objective: Ensure internal market functioning while enforcing a high level of protection for health, safety, fundamental rights (enshrined in the EU Charter), democracy, the rule of law, and environmental protection against harmful AI impacts.
  • Extraterritorial Reach: Applies to: 1. Providers placing AI systems or General-Purpose AI (GPAI) models on the market in the EU, regardless of whether established in the EU or a third country. 2. Deployers established or located within the EU. 3. Third-country providers and deployers where the output produced by the AI system is used within the EU.
  • Key Scope Exclusions: Military, defense, or national security operations; scientific research and development; pre-market research/testing activities (excluding real-world testing); purely personal non-professional activities by natural persons; and free/open-source AI models (unless classified as high-risk or prohibited under Article 5).

Key Statutory Definitions (Article 3)

  • AI System (Art. 3(1)): A machine-based system designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and exhibiting potential adaptiveness after deployment, inferring from input data how to generate outputs (predictions, content, recommendations, decisions) influencing physical or virtual environments.
  • General-Purpose AI (GPAI) Model (Art. 3(63)): An AI model—including those trained at scale using self-supervision—that displays significant generality, competently performs a wide range of distinct tasks, and can be integrated into diverse downstream applications.
  • Systemic Risk (Art. 3(65)): Risk specific to the high-impact capabilities of GPAI models, having widespread market impact or actual/foreseeable negative effects on public health, safety, security, fundamental rights, or society at scale across the value chain.
  • Deep Fake (Art. 3(60)): AI-generated or manipulated image, audio, or video content resembling existing persons, objects, places, or events that falsely appears authentic or truthful.
  • Serious Incident (Art. 3(49)): Malfunctioning leading to death/serious health harm, critical infrastructure disruption, fundamental rights infringements, or severe property/environmental damage.

Mandatory AI Literacy (Article 4)

Providers and deployers must implement proactive measures to foster AI literacy among their staff and personnel operating AI systems on their behalf. Education must be tailored to technical background, experience, context of use, and affected populations.

Special Category Personal Data Processing for Bias Mitigation (Article 4a)

By way of exception to GDPR / Regulation (EU) 2016/679 restrictions, Article 4a allows providers of high-risk AI systems (and under strict conditions, deployers and other providers) to process special categories of personal data (e.g., race, health, biometrics) solely for bias detection and correction, subject to strict cumulative safeguards: 1. Bias detection cannot be effectively achieved using synthetic, anonymized, or non-sensitive data. 2. Technical limits, state-of-the-art security, and pseudonymization are enforced. 3. Strict access documentation and confidentiality protections prevent unauthorized reuse. 4. Data is not transmitted or transferred to third parties. 5. Data is permanently erased once bias is corrected or retention periods expire. 6. Necessity and justification are documented in formal Records of Processing Activities (ROPA).

Prohibited AI Practices (Article 5)

The EU AI Act strictly prohibits placing on the market, putting into service, or using AI systems for the following practices:

┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ PROHIBITED AI PRACTICES (ART. 5) │
├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ • Subliminal, Manipulative, or Deceptive Behavioral Distortion │
│ • Exploitation of Age, Disability, or Social/Economic Vulnerabilities │
│ • Non-Consensual Explicit Material & Deep Fake Sexually Explicit Content │
│ • Social Scoring leading to Detrimental or Disproportionate Treatment │
│ • Criminal Risk Assessment based solely on Profiling/Personality Traits│
│ • Untargeted Scraping of CCTV/Web Images for Facial Recognition DBs │
│ • Workplace & Educational Emotion Recognition (Non-medical/safety) │
│ • Biometric Categorization inferring Sensitive Protected Attributes │
│ • Real-Time Remote Biometric Identification (RBI) in Public Law │
│ Enforcement (with strict statutory law enforcement exceptions) │
└────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  1. Subliminal / Manipulative Systems (Art. 5(1)(a)): Deployment of techniques beyond conscious awareness to distort behavior, appreciably impairing decision-making causing significant harm.
  2. Exploitative Systems (Art. 5(1)(b)): Exploiting vulnerabilities tied to age, disability, or socio-economic status to distort behavior causing significant harm.
  3. Non-Consensual Intimate Material & CSAM (Art. 5(1)(ba), (bb)): Generating or manipulating realistic intimate images, explicit sexual material without consent, or child sexual abuse material.
  4. Social Scoring (Art. 5(1)(c)): Evaluating/classifying natural persons over time based on social behavior or personality traits, leading to detrimental treatment in unrelated contexts or disproportionate punishment.
  5. Predictive Policing Profiling (Art. 5(1)(d)): Making criminal risk assessments of individuals based solely on profiling or personality traits (excluding human assessment supported by objective, verifiable facts).
  6. Untargeted Facial Scraping (Art. 5(1)(e)): Creating/expanding facial recognition databases via untargeted scraping of CCTV footage or internet images.
  7. Workplace & Educational Emotion Recognition (Art. 5(1)(f)): Inferring emotions in workplace or educational settings, except for strictly medical or safety reasons.
  8. Biometric Categorization of Protected Attributes (Art. 5(1)(g)): Categorizing individuals using biometric data to deduce race, political opinions, trade union membership, religious/philosophical beliefs, sex life, or sexual orientation.
  9. Real-Time Remote Biometric Identification (RBI) in Public Spaces for Law Enforcement (Art. 5(1)(h)): Prohibited in publicly accessible spaces unless strictly necessary for: * Targeted search for victims of abduction, human trafficking, or sexual exploitation, and missing persons. * Preventing an imminent, substantial threat to life/safety or a genuine, foreseeable terrorist attack. * Localization/identification of a suspect for serious crimes punishable by a custodial sentence of at least 4 years. * Requirement: Must complete a Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (FRIA) and register in the EU database prior to use (except in emergency situations where registration is completed post-hoc).

Answer Key

  1. Answer: The four core functions are GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, and MANAGE.   2. Answer: Under Article 2(1)(c), the EU AI Act applies to providers and deployers established in a third country outside the EU if the output produced by the AI system is used within the Union.   3. Answer: Explainability refers to a representation of the underlying technical mechanisms and algorithmic operations driving an AI system's functionality (answering "how" a decision was made).   4.   5. Answer: The three major categories of bias are systemic bias (historical, societal, or organizational norms and practices), computational and statistical bias (unrepresentative sample data, data quality issues, or algorithmic errors), and human-cognitive bias (perceptual errors and heuristics introduced by humans designing, operating, or relying on system outputs).
  6. Answer: Real-time RBI is permitted for law enforcement only when strictly necessary for: (1) targeted searches for specific victims of abduction, human trafficking, sexual exploitation, or missing persons; (2) preventing specific, substantial, and imminent threats to life or physical safety, or genuine, foreseeable terrorist attacks; or (3) localizing/identifying suspects of serious crimes punishable by at least four years of imprisonment.
  7. Answer: Traditional software risks are generally static and governed by predictable code structures, whereas AI systems rely on data that continuously changes, causing data, model, and concept drift over time. Furthermore, AI scale and opacity (billions/trillions of decision points) produce inscrutable emergent properties, making failure modes hard to predict, test, or reproduce using standard software engineering controls.
  8. Answer: In the ISO/IEC 42001 crosswalk, NIST GOVERN 1.6 maps directly to Annex B resource management controls: B.4.2 (Resource documentation), B.4.3 (Data resources), B.4.4 (Tooling resources), B.4.5 (System and computing resources), and B.4.6 (Human resources).
  9. Answer: A GPAI Model is an AI model trained on large data volumes using self-supervision at scale that displays significant generality and can competently perform a wide range of distinct downstream tasks. Systemic Risk is a risk specific to the high-impact capabilities of advanced GPAI models that can cause significant market disruption or negative impacts on public health, safety, security, fundamental rights, or society at scale.
  10. Answer: Block 4 ("Teach It") is a 30-minute block where the learner must explain the core concepts aloud or in writing, passing a 5-minute teach-back without notes.
